The Raw Material

Two Statements, Two Kinds of Number

Income Statement: A Movie of the Year

It adds up everything that happened over the whole fiscal year: sales coming in, product costs and operating costs going out, and the profit left over. Sales, COGS, SG&A and net income are all income statement numbers.

Balance Sheet: A Photo on One Day

It shows what the company owns and owes on the last day of the fiscal year. Inventory, receivables, payables, PP&E and total assets are all balance sheet numbers.

Why Ratios Mix Them

Most supply chain ratios divide a balance sheet number by an income statement number. That tells you how much the company had to hold (the photo) to produce a year of activity (the movie). Inventory days, for example, compares inventory on hand with a full year of cost of goods sold.

Annual figures for US public companies are in the 10-K filing, Item 8 (Financial Statements), on SEC EDGAR. A company's fourth-quarter earnings release, filed as an 8-K, usually reports the same consolidated statements sooner.

Before You Compare Companies

Five Checks That Prevent Wrong Answers

CheckWhy It Matters
Align the Fiscal YearsMany retailers end their year in late January or early February, while others end in December. Pair the years that cover the most overlapping months, and say when year-ends differ.
Watch for 53-Week YearsA 52-53 week calendar adds an extra week every five or six years. That year's sales look about 2% bigger, and the next year's growth looks weaker.
Use the Same UnitsFilings report in thousands or millions. Convert everything to one unit before dividing.
Match Cost to CostInventory and payables are recorded at cost, so divide them by COGS. Receivables are recorded at selling price, so divide them by sales.
Read the NotesAcquisitions, leases and where depreciation is reported all change the ratios. The footnotes explain each one.
